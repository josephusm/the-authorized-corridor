# The Myth of Transparency

After the scanner always comes the promise of transparency.

It is a polite promise. It no longer has the hard face of the gate. It says: look, everything is traced. It says: every passage has a log, every document a signature, every artifact a provenance, every decision an explanation. It says: if you are afraid of the machine, we will open the panel. We will show you the chain. We will give you a report. We will grant you a right of access. We will let you see enough that you can stop suspecting.

The problem is that, by then, the world has already passed through the scanner.

Transparency often arrives after the corridor's first political operation: deciding which reality has become visible. It can illuminate the path of what entered the format, but it does not restore what the format left outside. It is like turning on a perfect light inside a windowless room and calling it dawn. You see everything the room contains. The trick is that the room was not the world.

This does not mean transparency is useless. Without record, custody, and forum, not even the minimal possibility of contesting remains. Anyone who has lived through enough bureaucracy knows that pure darkness frees no one. Darkness favors whoever already owns the corridor. A system without traces is not more human; it is only more unaccountable. The question is not whether seeing is better than not seeing. The question is what you are seeing, from what height, and who decided before you that this was the scene.

Provenance is the exemplary case.

In theory, provenance promises origin. Every component, every contribution, every passage should say where it comes from. A clean chain should prevent fraud, concealment, contamination. It is a sensible promise, and precisely for that reason a dangerous one. The corridor works well when it uses real needs. No one wants artifacts without origin, models without datasets, decisions without a responsible actor, parcels without a sender. The point is that provenance can become an elegant form of shifting opacity.

Opaque origin, exposed signature.

The chain does not always reconstruct the real process. Sometimes it builds a terminal of attribution. You do not really know how the output was born, which mediations it crossed, which exclusions made it presentable, which hands adjusted it, which pieces of world were compressed because they did not fit the category. But you know who signed. You know which account approved. You know which document declares compliance. You know where to attach responsibility when the system needs a solid point.

It is a subtle difference only on paper. In practice it is enormous. Testimony recounts a process; the signature closes an object. Testimony can say: this happened, but the context is missing, the log lies, the exception was treated as noise, the criterion was wrong. The signature says: this version passes. The system does not ask for memory. It asks for ratification.

Here the myth of transparency reveals itself for what it is: not a simple lie, but a substitution. It promises access to truth and delivers access to the verifiable surface. The surface can be real. It can be useful. It can contain evidence. But it does not coincide with the full causal chain. It is the authorized front of the chain.

When a platform asks for human-in-the-loop, it is often not returning authority to human judgment. It is illuminating the human point of responsibility. When a system hides the operational origin of an output and then demands a human signature on the result, it does not increase truth. It increases grip. The machine remains opaque enough not to testify; the human becomes visible enough to pay.

Transparency, in these cases, does not dissolve the corridor. It makes it citable.

The same movement applies to institutional opacity. When a reform opens one level, opacity does not necessarily disappear. It descends. The political level becomes legible, the economic one remains turbid. The public protocol improves, procurement becomes more tangled. The interface becomes clear, the risk model remains proprietary. The user receives an explanation, but the criterion that decides which explanation is valid remains outside their range. Reform consumes attention and moral credit. After obtaining the transparent panel, it becomes harder to say that the engine behind the panel is still sealed.

This is the vertical migration of opacity. It does not require sabotage. It only requires finite attention.

The corridor grants light where light no longer wounds it. It is an old art. Showing the door after deciding who may approach. Showing the form after deciding which bodies have a field. Showing the log after deciding which events deserved to be logged. Showing the explanation after deciding what counts as an explanation.

The right to explanation looks like a rupture. And it can be one, in precise cases. If it produces a nameable author, a contestable record, and a forum capable of imposing cost, then it is not only theater. A procedure that creates these three elements does not automatically wash the blow; it can open a wound in the corridor. It can make lying costly. It can turn a private complaint into public contestation. We should not throw this away because we enjoy playing tough. That would be stupidity in a leather jacket.

But the right to explanation also has a dirtier side: it can expand the chain into territory that had not yet been captured. To ask for an explanation you often have to describe yourself in the format. You have to identify yourself, produce documents, name the harm according to admissible categories, accept the language of appeal, stay inside the channel. The subject who asks for transparency enters more deeply into the system they wanted to contest. The chain does not stop in front of them. It incorporates them as another link.

This is where transparency becomes recolonization.

Not because knowing is bad. Because certain forms of knowing are also forms of grip. The corridor does not only want to hide. It wants to produce a world in which even contestation increases the granularity of control. The question "why did you classify me this way?" can become the occasion to collect more data, refine the category, stabilize the threshold, train the future appeal. The subject asks to be seen better. The system responds by seeing them more, but not necessarily by listening to them.

Explanation has teeth only when it cracks the grain of the sensor. If it remains inside the existing grain, it is interface maintenance. It tells you which category ate you, not why that category had the right to bite.

The myth of transparency lives precisely in this confusion between seeing and contesting. A dashboard is not a forum. A log is not custody. A signature is not testimony. An explanation is not a shared scene. A report is not a judgment. The corridor loves these substitutions because they look like close relatives of justice. They have the right posture, the right light, the right vocabulary. Only the annoying piece is missing: someone who can impose cost on the device when the device lies.

Epistemic packaging adds another twist.

So far we have treated packaging as interface: a proprietary wrapper that hides labor, residue, materials, hands, wet cardboard. But the wrapper does not cover only cost. It also covers the leap in premise. If the front is coherent enough, an enormous claim can travel as a secondary detail. Surface verification checks continuity, tone, container identity, familiarity of frame. It does not measure the scale of the premise that has been slipped inside.

It happens in technical systems and it happens in texts. An analysis can hold together verifiable facts, recognizable sources, sober vocabulary, and then pass through the middle an out-of-scale assertion as if it were part of the same package. The surface does not tremble. The reader is already inside the interpretive corridor: anti-imperialism, national security, innovation, accountability, protection of minors, administrative efficiency. Once the corridor has been accepted, certain premises do not enter as theses to be proven. They enter as furniture.

This is one of the more mature forms of blindness. Not the shouted lie. Not propaganda you can smell from twenty meters away. The packaged premise. The enormous claim that does not ask for entry because it arrives together with smaller objects, already authorized. Like a parcel with documents in order and altered contents. The inspection checks the label, the weight, the sender, the continuity of transport. It does not open the logical leap.

The trash bin, afterward, preserves something. Remains of packaging, scraps, contradictions, sentences that do not match, the smell of glue. But residue, too, needs someone to read it. Waste can become a dirty archive only if there are custody, forum, and time to rummage. Otherwise it remains wet cardboard: material evidence without a probative circuit. The corridor does not have to eliminate every clue. It is enough to offload the cost of reading onto whoever has the least strength to bear it.

Transparency can even worsen this point. The more documented the package is, the more unfair it seems to suspect it. There is tracking. There is the signature. There is the policy. There is the accountability page. There is the standard explanation. There is the appeal form. Everything is visible, therefore everything seems handled. Suspicion becomes procedural bad manners.

This is the moral function of the myth: to make whoever looks underneath seem paranoid.

And yet the counterexample remains important. Not every procedure is laundering. Not every request for transparency is capture. If a procedure produces a nameable author, preserves a contestable record, and opens a forum capable of imposing cost, then it can break the corridor at least at one point. The difference is brutal and concrete. Can I know who decided? Can I see enough of the causal chain to contest it? Is there someone who can force the system to pay for the error, not merely explain it to me in a calm voice?

If one of these three elements is missing, transparency risks being theater. Not always useless, but theater. A theater can still reveal something, of course. Even stage sets have real nails. But it should not be confused with a tribunal.

The myth of transparency, then, is not that "seeing everything" is false. It is that seeing everything inside an already authorized grid is mistaken for seeing the world. The chain of provenance shows the admitted origin. The signature shows the available responsible actor. The log shows the event the sensor recognized. The explanation shows the criterion the system agrees to name. Packaging shows a surface continuity smooth enough to carry even disproportionate premises.

What remains outside is the rest: unrecorded labor, the category that did not know how to smell, the harm below threshold, the residue without custody, testimony transformed into signature, the packaged premise, the cost of reading offloaded elsewhere.

The corridor does not fear transparency when transparency arrives as internal illumination of its format. Often, in fact, it desires it. A machine seen from inside its own interface looks more honest than a hidden machine. And a machine that looks honest more easily obtains the right to close the loop.

This is the next passage.

First the corridor selects what can be seen. Then it promises transparency on what it selected. Then it uses that transparency as proof of reliability. At that point human intervention becomes friction, not a guarantee. If everything is traced, explained, signed, verified, why still leave an opening for living decision? Why tolerate the delay of judgment? Why wait for a custodian who might dissent?

The blindness of verification does not end in transparency. Transparency prepares it for its cleanest form: the closed loop.

There, it is no longer necessary to hide much.

It is enough not to leave time.
