# When the Corridor Closes

Part III ended with an uncomfortable question: what recognition is already turning me into food?

Here begins the most dangerous stretch of the book, because the easy answer is also the most seductive: then do not let yourself be recognized. Leave the format. Become illegible. Withdraw from the scanner. Leave the corridor with nothing to process.

There is some truth in this. Enough that it cannot be discarded. Not enough to turn it into a liturgy.

When the corridor closes, legible resistance enters a toxic zone. Every request for admission confirms the corridor’s right to decide what may be admitted. Every appeal accepts, at least provisionally, a forum that may itself be part of the problem. Every authorized protest already bears the grammar of its own management. Every claimed identity can become a category, target, audience, segment, risk, evidence, narrative package.

This does not mean that appeals, protests, claims, and identities are useless. That would be the usual radical pose that confuses the purity of the gesture with the cost paid by other people. If an appeal gives you back your wages, voice, permit, home, or safety, it is not a theoretical trap: it is a piece of life. The point is different. When the corridor is already closed, the question is no longer only whether a gesture works inside the format. It is whether that success strengthens the format that made the gesture necessary.

The corridor’s closure does not always appear as prohibition. It often appears as saturation. Every door exists, but each one opens onto a form. Every right exists, but each one requires proof in the language of the institution. Every criticism can be heard, but only if it arrives as feedback, case, risk, review, ticket, or stakeholder input. The system does not say: be silent. It says: speak here, at this length, in this tone, with this attachment, within this window, after accepting that the problem belongs to a type I know how to receive.

At that point recognition becomes a form of consumption.

Not consumption in the melodramatic sense. Something more ordinary, therefore worse. The recognized gesture feeds reports. The accusation feeds training. The refusal feeds risk mapping. Presence feeds legitimation. Absence feeds inference. The signature feeds provenance. Contestation feeds proof that the system listens. The corridor does not have to love what it receives; it only has to be able to metabolize it.

This is the first meaning of closure: not lack of entry, but the absence of any entry that cannot be metabolized.

The promise of obsolescence shows the shape clearly. The classic immunosuppressant of work used to say: you can always leave. If the job is bad, change companies, change industries, change platforms, improve your profile. It is a promise of exit: false or partial, but still built around the idea that the subject has a function and can move it elsewhere.

The promise of obsolescence says something colder: you will have no function. You are not being exploited badly; you are expiring. You are not offered an exit; you are told that the future corridor will no longer need you as a vertical subject. This produces a brutal fork. Where a constituency is already organized, the threat can catalyze resistance. Hollywood was able to turn AI into an object of bargaining because it already had unions, memory, leverage, and precedents. Where the constituency is still emerging, the same threat dissolves the ground before it can become collective. In video games, in many kinds of cultural work, in freelance and quasi-creative zones, obsolescence arrives before the base has developed the muscle to name itself.

The same pressure, two opposite outcomes. Nice way for a device to work: it does not even have to choose. It only has to apply the same temperature to different substrates and let structure do the rest.

When the corridor closes, then, it does not only close the door. It closes the useful time in which political constituencies can form. It lets you speak once you are already a category, negotiate once you are already replaceable, protest once your presence has already become historical data or reputational risk. Legible resistance arrives at the table with its own obsolescence already written on the menu.

This is where the temptation of disconnection begins.

If the corridor has jurisdiction over relational resources, then resources have to be taken outside the relationship. A book instead of a feed. A local model instead of an API. A downloaded map instead of a service. Knowledge that can be accumulated instead of a subscription. Open weights instead of a remote box. The gesture is not nostalgic. It is technical. A resource that works without a connection exposes less surface to revocation, ranking, dynamic pricing, and silent changes to terms.

Here the prepper, a figure often mocked in the culture of the commons, sees something that connected moralism tends to miss: what you cannot use while disconnected is not yours in any robust sense. You may have paid for it, loved it, cited it, indexed it, but if its operation depends on an ongoing relationship with the carrier, the corridor remains in the room.

Yet this move too has an edge. Functional disconnection is not enough if it remains tied to the corridor’s metric. Bitcoin is the cleanest case, precisely because it carries the loudest promise of independence. You can keep it offline, remove it from banks, transact without asking permission from many institutions. But if you continue to measure it in dollars, if its victories and defeats are still narrated by the fiat price feed, the corridor’s evaluative jurisdiction reaches you with the cable unplugged. You have removed operational control, not the unit of account.

The distinction is blunt: functional disconnection is not metric disconnection.

A good with direct use value—food, tools, local knowledge, a model that actually runs on your machine, a library you can read without logging in—can be affected by the outside world, but it does not have to ask the price feed whether it exists. An asset whose value lives mainly in market recognition remains suspended from the corridor’s language. Even when it sleeps in cold storage, it dreams in dollars.

So a closed corridor cannot be broken by a single withdrawal. It has different tentacles: operational, metric, symbolic, legal, reputational. Cut one off and the others may continue to feed.

The ugliest tentacle is the one that assigns presence without asking you for it.

So far I have often written as if the subject could choose whether to enter, sign, withdraw, participate, or become legible. But some corridors do not wait for your presence before reusing it. They manufacture it. Origin, name, kinship, language, passport, diaspora, address, school, travel, contact, affiliation: any of these can become operational evidence before any gesture is made. In the security frame, presence is not granted. It is ascribed.

This breaks the comforting fable of withdrawal. If a category of suspicion is assigned, refusing to take part in the ritual is not enough. The corridor already owns a version of you that is good enough for its purpose. It does not need you to speak: the record speaks. It does not need you to sign: the profile signs. It does not need you to subscribe: the pattern subscribes in your place.

This is where illegibility becomes ambiguous. It can protect you when the system needs your live signal. It can fail when the system has enough cold residue to replace you. Worse: your withdrawal may leave the field precisely to that residue. Archive, lander, storefront, dead domain, old profile, screenshot, metadata, institutional page left stale. The living close themselves off to defend against machines and discover that the most indexable corpse is all that remains to speak in public.

This is the point that must not be romanticized. Pure non-reusability can become political invisibility. And political invisibility is not silence. It is involuntary delegation to the cold layer.

What is needed, then, is a figure poorer than the great disappearance and more concrete than full participation: the minimal opening.

A small trace, not a sacrificial offering. Poor enough not to become easy raw material. Verifiable enough not to let archive and metadata speak alone. Contestable enough to keep a forum open. Not a dashboard of the self. Not a performative profile. Not presence granted to every scanner in the name of transparency. A cold label attached to a warm friction: author, date, defect, consequence, responsibility, means of challenge.

This minimal opening does not redeem the corridor. It is not an elegant solution, and I distrust elegant solutions as much as knives that are too shiny. It is a harm-reduction tactic against two opposite captures: on one side full legibility, which turns presence into a license for reuse; on the other total closure, which leaves the residues most convenient to the system to do the speaking.

If illegible resistance wants to survive without becoming superstition, it has to learn this dirty geometry. Not everything visible is captured in the same way. Not everything hidden is free. Not every trace is collaboration. Not every absence is withdrawal. The question becomes: what trace remains, in whose hands, inside which forum, at what cost to those who misuse it?

When the corridor closes, the problem is not simply to leave. Leaving may be impossible, insufficient, or already translated into another field in the database. The problem is to strip the corridor of its quiet right to decide which version of you, the harm, the work, or the conflict will continue to circulate.

Sometimes this requires disconnection. Sometimes legible organization. Sometimes a local object. Sometimes a reconstructed handle. Sometimes the withdrawal of the body. Sometimes a minimal trace left deliberately, like a nail in the wall: little material, serious consequences if someone tries to tear it out without leaving a name.

Part IV will follow these forms without turning them into holy cards. Vacancy, lying flat, re-discretization, the tired ribosome, the internal gap: these are not roads to salvation. They are different ways in which the corridor’s grip loses continuity. Some are strategies, others conditions, still others side effects. All can be recaptured if they are mistaken for purity.

The first passage is vacancy. Not generic absence, not burnout made into a pose, not inactivity narrated as sabotage so we can sleep better. Vacancy is more material and meaner than that: the substrate that stops holding the posture the corridor knows how to read. The body does not resist. It lowers itself. And by lowering itself, it takes away the verticality on which the device had built its grip.
