# The Epistemological Scapegoat

The previous chapter ended with a convenient and poisonous figure: a target real enough to absorb anger, partial enough to leave the corridor intact.

It is not a straw man. That point has to remain firm, otherwise the concept becomes a conspiracy theory in a good suit. The epistemological scapegoat is not necessarily false, nor is it always invented by someone in a closed room. It is real. It causes harm. It deserves scrutiny. Sometimes it also deserves punishment. But its role in the system is to focus attention on a visible object while the mechanism that makes it useful, desirable, or recurrent keeps working behind it.

The classical scapegoat takes social blame. The epistemological one takes visibility. It is not only blamed: it is made into the place where public knowledge can stop without feeling cowardly. Once we have named it, it seems that we have understood. Once we have regulated it, it seems that we have acted. Once we have sacrificed it, it seems that we have touched the system.

Sometimes that is true. Often it is not.

The case that gave me the rough shape of the concept was simple, almost too clean: a visible technology absorbs criticism while the invisible infrastructure stays in place. The test, the model, the chatbot, the detector, the scoring module, the product name. Public debate attaches itself there because there is an outline there. There is a screen. There is an output. There is a brand. There is a thing that can be photographed, insulted, banned, corrected.

The operational mechanism, by contrast, has no face. Legacy databases, incentives, contracts, pipelines, rankings, metrics, triage procedures, access costs, outsourcing, chains of delegation. None of this gives anger a good face. The corridor does not have to hide it theatrically. It only has to make it easier to talk about the object in front than about the structure behind it.

Here the inherited signifier does another job. Words such as “accountability,” “safety,” “oversight,” “appeal,” “review,” and “transparency” continue to look like bridges toward the mechanism. In many cases they are only gangways toward the scapegoat. Not because they mean nothing. Because they mean enough to stop the question before it reaches the right layer.

If a platform wrongly removes a piece of content, the content is the immediate target. Then the decision. Then the moderator, human or automated. Then the classifier. Then the policy. Every layer can be criticized. Every layer can also be improved. But the question that matters is drier: how far up does the cost travel?

An appeal that restores a post corrects the payload. Useful, certainly. If you are the person affected, this is not a theoretical subtlety. The payload is your voice, your work, your income, perhaps your safety. It should not be dismissed from the height of a fine sentence about the carrier. Systemic cynicism often begins exactly this way: by calling someone’s concrete life a “particular.”

But the book is not asking whether correcting the payload is useless. It is asking whether that correction bites the carrier. And the carrier has to be cut into layers, not named as a mystical block.

There is the enforcement layer: rules, notices, strikes, response times. There is the classifier layer: which signals turn an action into a violation. There is the human-review layer: who looks, under what mandate, with how much time, at what mental wage. There is the distribution layer: ranking, reach, demotion, amplification. There is the economic layer: advertising, payouts, revenue sharing, incentives to produce or tolerate harm. There is the triage layer: who can even reach the forum without being consumed by forms, identity checks, CAPTCHAs, reputation, language, time.

Saying “the forum touches the carrier” is not enough. Which layer does it touch? Does it change that layer permanently? Does it impose a cost? Does it reduce its fitness? Does it shift the distribution of harm? Or does it produce a local correction that the system can reabsorb as proof of its own maturity?

That is the grid. Inconvenient, but at least it does not cheat.

Take Meta’s Oversight Board as a partial counterexample. It is not pure smoke. It has produced decisions, recommendations, and real pressure. It can correct enforcement. It can push changes to notices, rules, and review criteria. It can expose inconsistencies. It can force Meta to respond publicly, and that is already something. Where there used to be only a flat door, at least a surface of friction appears.

But the high ground remains elsewhere. If the Board cannot truly see the algorithms of amplification and demotion, if it cannot impose lasting changes on ranking, reach, and advertising incentives, then its forum remains strong on some layers and weak on others. This is not a total failure. It is a map of partial bite. The epistemological scapegoat appears precisely when the system allows controversy to exhaust itself on the correctable layers while the fitness layers keep producing the world in which those controversies become necessary.

The DSA’s out-of-court dispute-settlement bodies show the same cut. They can be useful. They can provide a forum. They can require a platform to reconsider a decision. But if they remain confined to content moderation in the individual case, if they are non-binding, if they do not travel upward toward recommenders, advertising, and incentive design, then they are not where the carrier pays. They are a downstream valve. Necessary, perhaps. But still downstream.

Article 35 of the DSA, with its systemic risk assessment, seems to come closer to the right point: recommenders, advertising, civic effects, manipulation, the protection of rights. Here the forum does not look only at the post that was removed or left online. It looks at the machine that makes certain forms of harm profitable, scalable, or invisible. Yet even here we have to avoid procedural superstition. A risk assessment is not automatically gravity. It becomes gravity only if it produces an obligation, a loss, a modification, friction, a redistribution of cost. Otherwise it is another document with excellent posture.

This distinction saves the concept from its lazy version. The epistemological scapegoat is not universal. Not every visible object drains accountability. Sometimes it opens it up.

The Boeing 737 MAX shows this clearly. MCAS was a visible, nameable device, almost perfect scapegoat material: an automated system, an acronym, a technical defect on which to hang two disasters. But the controversy did not stop there. It traveled upward to certification, FAA delegation, ODA, grandfathering, Boeing’s post-merger culture, and the relationship between commercial pressure and safety. MCAS became a gateway, not a drain. The visible target led into the invisible mechanism.

Cambridge Analytica worked in a similar way. The quiz, the app, the company with a second-rate thriller for a name: everything was ready to absorb blame and close the scene. Instead attention fell on Facebook’s data ecosystem, third-party harvesting, fictitious consent, political advertising, and regulation. Not enough, probably. But enough to show that a potential scapegoat can turn into a door.

Volkswagen and the defeat device add another variation. The cheating software was the visible object. It could have been treated as technical deviance: a cheat, a department, a line of code. Instead it exposed industrial fraud, regulatory failures, inadequate testing, and an industry culture. Here too the visible device did not fully protect the carrier. It punctured it.

So the question is not: is there a visible object? The question is: what institutional architecture exists around that visible object?

If there are independent investigators with a mandate, access, and power, the visible can become a gateway. If there are courts, genuine technical audits, protected whistleblowers, media capable of moving past the first face, regulators with teeth and memory, the scapegoat can fail as a scapegoat. It is taken by the horns and pulled all the way to the mechanism. It happens. Not as often as it should, but it happens.

If, on the other hand, the forum is weak, captured, fragmented, or confined to the individual case, the visible becomes a drain. The system offers a point for indignation and lets indignation exhaust itself there. It changes the form. Suspends a contractor. Renames a policy. Updates a classifier. Adds an appeal. Publishes a report. All of these may be good things. All of them are insufficient if the harm lives higher up or further down.

The epistemological scapegoat is therefore conditional. It requires a difference in visibility between layers and a weakness in the forum capable of traveling upward. Where the forum rises, the scapegoat stops working. Where the forum remains downstream, the scapegoat thrives.

This conditional form is less elegant than a universal law. Too bad. Universal laws are often just laziness in a clean jacket.

One hard fact remains: modern systems are good at producing visible objects precisely on the layers where cost is easiest to absorb. The interface is visible. The classifier becomes visible when it fails. The moderator is visible when they sign. The model is visible when it spits out an obscene sentence. The dashboard is visible when it changes color. Ranking, by contrast, appears as environment. The incentive appears as market. Triage appears as necessity. The payout appears as neutral infrastructure. The cost of access appears as security.

And what appears as environment is always harder to accuse.

This is where the front door and the appeal meet. At the front, the system cools the entry point: CAPTCHAs, institutional shells, dead pages, anti-bot walls, required accounts, feeds readable only from a distance. At the back, once the harm has already occurred, it offers calm, modular, often courteous contestation. They seem like opposites: interdiction first, openness later. In fact, they are the same architecture seen from opposite ends. On entry it decides who can become a legible subject. On exit it decides which harm can become a correctable case.

If the forum is to count, it has to become gravity. Being a room is not enough. It has to bend the path of the carrier. It has to impose cost on the layers that produce fitness: distribution, incentives, access, triage, payout—not only the individual decision. It has to make those who control the format lose something when the format causes harm. If it does not, it remains a courteous window. Little air, good view, locked door.

This also explains why contemporary triage is political. In a bug bounty flooded with generated reports, in the maintainer who receives slop alongside useful contributions, in the system that introduces background checks and validation agents, the problem is not only technical quality. It is who can be heard without consuming the forum. When noise grows, the system does not simply become more open or more closed. It becomes selective in a new way. The cognitive cost of discernment turns into an architecture of access.

At that point the scapegoat may be “AI slop,” “abusive users,” “spam,” “misinformed activists,” “incompetent moderators,” or an “opaque algorithm.” Each of these targets may be real. Each can also protect the uglier question: who built a forum that collapses as soon as it has to listen to too many people?

The opposite temptation is romantic: if the system captures every admissible criticism, then all we have to do is become illegible. Disappear, lie flat, make noise, refuse the category. Part IV will have to cross this temptation without kneeling before it. Because illegibility breaks some corridors, but does not guarantee an exit from the database. A public that withdraws does not produce only silence; it produces residue. Passive traces, patterns, absences, exceptions, waste. Even absence can become material for triage.

Before that, however, Part III has to close with a more sober threshold. The epistemological scapegoat does not say that every criticism is futile. It says that criticism has to ask where it lands. If it lands on the visible target and produces no cost on the layer that makes that target recurrent, the corridor has won even when it admits the wrong. If, instead, the visible target becomes a ladder toward the mechanism, then the scapegoat fails and the forum begins to bite.

The difference does not lie in the purity of the criticism. It lies in the architecture that receives it.

This is the bridge toward illegible resistance. Not because illegible automatically means free. But because, when the corridor decides in advance which targets can be seen, resistance’s first question is no longer, “How do I make myself recognizable?” It is more dangerous: “What recognition is already turning me into food?”
