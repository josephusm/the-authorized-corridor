# Admissibility vs Transitability

Imagine a port.

Not the romantic port, with cranes on the horizon and the sea doing what the sea has always done. A contemporary port: containers, badges, cameras, sensors, automatic gates, clearance lists, insurance, risk profiles, systems that decide before the human being and then leave the human to sign the decision. A body arrives in front of the scanner. It is not stopped by a wall. That would be too simple, and mature systems hate simplicity when it can leave behind a recognizable culprit.

The scanner does something cleaner. It decomposes.

It does not see a body, it sees components. Contradictions. Origins. Probabilities. Thresholds. Already validated names. Administrative statuses. Risks. Exceptions. Missing fields. Every part passes through a grid that does not ask: can this person cross? It first asks: can this person become the kind of object the system knows how to handle?

This is the difference between transitability and admissibility.

Transitability is the visible question: can you pass from here to there? Do you have the document, the permit, the signature, the code, the proof, the required relationship? It is the level that produces the administrative scene: yes, no, waiting, appeal, receipt. Transitability looks political because it decides movement. But it arrives late.

Admissibility works before that. It decides what can enter the format as a recognizable object. It does not ask whether your case deserves protection; it asks whether there is a form of case that can contain you. It does not ask whether your speech is true; it asks whether it can appear as valid speech in the registry. It does not ask whether the harm is real; it asks whether the sensor knows how to hear it as an event.

If the answer is no, you are not properly rejected. Rejection would still be a relation. You are left outside the world in which rejection can be named.

This is the first trick of the authorized corridor: making the problem look like a door, when the problem is the kind of body that can arrive in front of the door.

A category is not a neutral container. It is a sensor. In the administered world, there is no pure fact first and innocent classification afterward. Classification already decides what kind of fact can appear. If the sensor searches for declared war, natural disaster, fraud, market, or policing as separate columns, a harm that crosses those columns remains below threshold even when it is full of bodies.

Take the dirty example, not the textbook one. A community is pushed out of its territory by illegal mining, logging, drug routes, armed groups, environmental degradation, economic blackmail, and institutional failure. The harm is not missing. Causality is not missing. Violence is not missing either. What is missing is the column that knows how to sense the entanglement as a full event. Every office sees one piece: environment, security, illegal market, migration, public order, development. Each piece is too small to become catastrophe inside its own registry. The whole is enormous, but the whole has no field.

The blood is there. It is the machine's nose that does not smell it.

Here the corridor does not censor. It does not need to. Censorship at least recognizes the existence of what it suppresses. The most efficient corridor organizes the world so that certain things never reach the point where a no would be necessary. It produces a perceptual field. It decides which fumes can become fire, which complaints can become cases, which bodies can become subjects, which residues can become cost.

That is why admissibility is more radical than transitability. Transitability can be negotiated inside the system: appeal, exception, review, escalation, countersignature. Admissibility decides whether there is anything to negotiate. When it is missing, even the appeal becomes a room with no handle. You can shout as much as you want; the microphone is not switched off, it is pointed somewhere else.

Foucault sees an ancient form of the same device in the crisis of parrhesia. Formally, in the democratic scene, everyone can speak. Practically, the one who adheres to the audience's desire is the one who gets heard. The filter is not truth, it is desirability. The flatterer passes because he confirms the scene; the parrhesiast disturbs because he says something that exposes the price of the scene. It is not false. Worse: it is not listenable.

This point matters because it dismantles a very resilient liberal fantasy: the idea that opening the channel is enough. Giving voice is not enough if the device has already decided what kind of voice can become an event. An open form can be a refined form of closure. A public consultation can become decoration if there is no obligation to carry the residue into the decision. An audience can look pluralist while rewarding only the forms of truth that do not ask it to change posture.

The modern corridor automates that old selection. It no longer needs a crowd applauding flatterers and hissing at whoever breaks the spell. It can delegate selection to the format. It can ask for required fields, acceptable evidence, taxonomies, risk scores, temporal windows, verified profiles, compatible identities. It can say: speak freely. Then it keeps only what enters the right column.

This is where the misunderstanding of transparency begins. If the problem were only transitability, seeing the door better would be enough: who decides, under which rule, in how much time, with what possibility of appeal. All necessary things. Let us not throw them away as an anti-bureaucratic pose. Anti-bureaucratic poses are the fastest way to leave all power to those who already own the bureaucracy.

But if the problem is admissibility, transparency at the door is not enough. You can perfectly illuminate a gate built in front of the wrong corridor. You can know why your case was rejected and never discover why your harm did not become a case. You can receive an impeccable explanation inside a blind category. It is like receiving the report of an instrument calibrated to look for metal when the problem is toxic smoke. The report is precise. The corpse remains yours.

Explanation has teeth only when it cracks the seam between database and interface. Not when it adds a courtesy screen. Not when it produces a paragraph in natural language that translates the decision into the same ontology that made it possible. It is useful when it forces the system to declare its map: which columns it separated, which thresholds it chose, which signals it let decay, which types of harm could not even be aggregated.

The real question is not: why did you decide this way?

It is: what world do you have to presuppose in order to decide this way?

Here packaging enters. Caronia saw it clearly: packaging is not just waste around the commodity. It is the material support of imagination, trust, information, relationship. The package replaces a local network of trust with a controlled surface: brand, promise, warranty, language, seduction, instructions for use. It does not eliminate the social; it captures it and gives it back as a proprietary interface.

The corridor does the same with procedure. It packages meaning as long as meaning produces adhesion. It gives you a clean surface: dashboard, appeal, status, tracking, policy, wellness, self-audit, explanation. Everything looks like relationship. Everything looks like care. Then the residue arrives: the cost, the exclusion, the wet cardboard, the clogging, the contestation, the work of proving that harm is harm. At that point the package changes nature. Meaning was private; refusal becomes collective.

Old trick. New shrink-wrap.

This image also corrects one of my instincts, too easy by half. The enemy is not the interface as such. A world without interfaces is only a world where whoever already knows the hidden passages rules. The problem is the interface that sells relationship and then dumps the residue offstage. Proprietary packaging does not only hide the touch; it parks the waste where it cannot climb back to the sender. The blow arrives without a hand. The wet cardboard stays on everyone's landing.

A procedure is not bad because it is a procedure. It can even reopen the touch. When it produces a nameable author, a challengeable record, and a forum capable of imposing cost, the form stops being wallpaper and becomes an adversarial surface. An appeal that can really reverse a decision, a rationale that exposes the rule, a judge or body that can make the error expensive: these are not details. They are handles.

The point, then, is not to oppose life and form, truth and administration, body and procedure. That would be convenient. Too convenient, therefore almost certainly false. The point is to ask whether the procedure keeps three things in the same space: who touched, what was left behind, who can return the residue to the sender.

Without those three things, procedure launders the blow. With those three things, it can make the blow attackable.

The distinction between admissibility and transitability serves exactly here. A system can grant local transitability and deny structural admissibility. It can allow you to appeal only after turning you into the kind of subject its appeal knows how to handle. It can listen to your suffering only when it becomes risk, inefficiency, reputation, not when it remains pain. It can retain slow signals, near-misses, clogs, but make them spendable only if they return as leverage for the system itself.

The buffer does not save what it keeps. It spends only what it can use.

That is why many contemporary forms of self-audit look to me like the opposite of askesis. Self-examination, in the parrhesiastic line, prepares for risk: it makes the subject less blackmailable by the format, less eager to be compatible with the scene. Administrative self-audit uses the same surface posture — look at yourself, measure yourself, correct yourself — but bends it toward legibility and reuse. It does not produce someone capable of telling the truth at the cost of losing a place in the room. It produces someone who hands the room a cleaner version of themselves.

The corridor does not silence that truth. It repackages it as maturity.

At this point the body in front of the scanner has already changed. Not because it has been physically dismantled, though that happens too sometimes, under other names. It has changed because the system has decided which of its components can have formal value. One part becomes verified identity. One part becomes risk. One part becomes exception. One part becomes noise. One part becomes residue to be left outside the door.

The hand that comes out of the scanner can be recognized as a hand and no longer be able to grasp anything. It is the hand of sand: form present, agency evaporated. The system can say it admitted you, because a version of you entered. It can say it let you transit, because that version received an outcome. What it does not say is how much of you was discarded before the question began.

Resistance, inside this chapter, is not yet a romantic theory of the illegible. That will come later, and it will have to be careful not to become an aesthetics of defeat. Here it is enough to fix the lever: the authorized corridor decides before passage. The struggle does not begin in front of the gate, but in the calibration of the sensor, in the duration of the buffer, in the form of the interface, in the possibility of returning the residue to whoever produces it.

As long as we discuss only transitability, we ask the system for a better door.

When we discuss admissibility, we ask who built the world in which that door looks like the only real thing.

It is a less polite question. Good. Polite questions are often the packaging of refusal.
