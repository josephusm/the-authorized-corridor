# The Genealogy of the Tool

The scanner does not begin with the scanner.

This is the part the machine prefers to make us forget. Every mature device presents itself as inevitable: natural, technical, neutral, a piece of common sense someone has finally installed. Before, there was chaos; then the tool arrived. Before, there were human errors, frauds, ambiguous bodies, incomplete documents, slow decisions; then a grid restored order. The official history is always this one, because it serves to erase the dirty work of genealogy.

A tool of control rarely begins from an abstract desire to control. It begins from an escape.

Someone passes where they were not supposed to pass. Someone uses a fold in the system better than the one who built it. A cyberattack exploits a permission left too wide. A worker bypasses a procedure because they know the warehouse's real rhythm. A user keeps a copy when the service thought it had a monopoly over the document. A subject speaks in a form the audience had not expected and, for a moment, the word arrives. It is not revolution yet. Often it is only a crack. But cracks teach.

The corridor learns above all from successful escapes.

The failure of an escape confirms the existing procedure. Success, instead, forces the procedure to be rewritten. Every evasion leaves an involuntary map: here the gate was weak, here the field was not mandatory, here the custodian still had discretion, here the body could still present itself whole instead of decomposed into attributes. The institutional response is rarely to reduce control. More often it is to formalize the crack: turn it into a rule, a log, training, a signature, a risk criterion, a new layer of authorization.

That is how a successful escape propagates architecture. Not because it fails. Worse: because it works. The endogenous tool carries with it the material of the house it wanted to dismantle. A procedure born to protect can become the model for the next closure. A license conceived as defense can be bypassed by a legal plane that does not recognize derivation. A language born to name solidarity can become a grammar of capture. The corridor does not always have to destroy the tool of escape. It is enough for it to inherit it after success.

From here comes its first administrative virtue: formalization.

Formalization is sold as an antidote to arbitrariness. In part, it is. Living judgment can be corrupt, lazy, racist, capricious, self-interested. No nostalgia for the enlightened functionary saves us from this. The problem is that the corridor uses a partial truth as a total lever. It says: human judgment makes mistakes, so let us replace it with criteria. It says: performance is fragile, so let us turn it into a test. It says: what has no format is not reliable, so let us give it a format.

At that point the living practice loses its main force: not yet being entirely captured.

An act of empathy becomes a score. Care becomes a checklist. Competence becomes certification. A protest becomes a consultation. Testimony becomes a descriptive field. A practice of the self becomes self-audit. The corridor does not attack performance frontally; it promotes it into an administrable object. It gives it a name, a form, a metric. Then it measures the metric and calls that result reality.

It is an elegant gesture, therefore a dangerous one. It does not say: you cannot do this. It says: do it, by all means, but in the format that makes the doing comparable, archivable, replaceable. Performance, once formalized, no longer disturbs the system in the same way. It can be rewarded, corrected, scaled, sold. Living judgment is not abolished by decree. It is turned into an input of the machine.

Here the custodian's dilemma enters.

Every real infrastructure needs someone who knows it well enough to defend it. There are no honest systems without maintenance, and there is no maintenance without people with something at stake. The custodian knows where the bridge vibrates, where the database lies, where the procedure works only because someone bends it a little every day. They know which exceptions are healthy and which are corruption. They know how to distinguish noise from a crack.

Precisely for this reason they are dangerous.

To defend a system means to know its vulnerabilities. To know them means to be able to use them. Even if you will not use them, the mature system does not reason in terms of trust; it reasons in terms of attack surface. The custodian becomes a deposit of unauthorized possibilities. Their competence is both protection and threat. If they defend too early, they look paranoid. If they defend too late, they look responsible for the damage. If they defend well, they reveal that they saw something the system was officially not supposed to depend on their gaze in order to see.

The typical solution is stratification.

Knowledge is broken apart. Custody is distributed into modules. Each layer sees enough to perform its own task and too little to understand the whole. The lower level keeps logs, checksums, chains of provenance, raw signals. The upper level keeps signatures, nominal responsibilities, summary dashboards, approval power. In between there are translations: from body to data, from data to profile, from profile to risk, from risk to decision, from decision to rationale.

It looks like security. Often it is only well-organized opacity.

Stratification solves the custodian's dilemma by eliminating the whole custodian. No one has enough world in hand to betray the system anymore; no one has enough world in hand to save it either. Every piece can say it did its job. The sensor measured. The module classified. The interface displayed. The supervisor signed. The appeal followed the prescribed channel. The chain is clean because every link is locally clean. The fact that the chain as a whole strangled something belongs to no link.

The scanner is the compact form of this genealogy.

It is not only a machine that looks. It is a machine built to verify without judging. Its purpose is not to understand a body, a practice, a word. It is to decompose it into verifiable components. Provenance. Pattern. Anomaly. Document. Temperature. Face. Movement. Risk. Status. Compatibility. Every component enters a criterion. What does not enter is not refuted; it decays.

Decay is the trick. A wrong judgment leaves an adversary: someone judged badly, with a reason, a name, an error. Decay leaves much less. A signal below threshold was not rejected, not insulted, not condemned. It simply remained without event. Surprise becomes noise. Contradiction becomes risk. Unadministered unease becomes an empty field.

In the scanner dream the body was reassembled without the incompatible parts: 47% contradictions, 23% surprise, 30% unadministered unease. The figure does not matter as statistics; it matters as political anatomy. The scanner does not need to hate surprise. It only needs to lack a column in which surprise can remain surprise long enough to produce a question.

From here comes telemetry.

When the system no longer wants to depend on the custodian and no longer wants to risk judgment, it translates the world into continuous signals. The ship becomes a trace, the sea a spreadsheet, war a logistics counter, the body a profile, labor a rhythm, suffering an indicator of engagement or risk. Telemetry gives perceptual relief. It removes the burden of looking. You no longer have to ask yourself what stands in front of you; you only have to interpret a panel.

The panel is not necessarily false. This is the annoying part. It can be accurate. It can save lives. It can see things no human eye would see. But local accuracy does not guarantee contact. A drone can have precise coordinates and no longer have a scene. A healthcare system can have punctual metrics and no longer have a person. A corporate workflow can know how much time passes between two states and ignore completely the informal work that holds the states together. The problem is not the number. It is the world the number authorizes.

This genealogy is older than digital automation. Foucault, reading parrhesia, already shows a corridor without electronic scanners: everyone can speak, but the audience selects what it wants to hear. The true speech that puts the speaker at risk is replaced by speech compatible with the scene. Technical modernity does not invent this selection; it frees it from the nuisance of listening. There is no longer any need to wait for the demos to reward the flatterer. The format can do it first, silently, through a grammar of admissibility.

The new step is that selection disguises itself as an object.

The artificial double, the automaton, AI, the speaking machine: all convenient figures because they pull the gaze toward the smooth face. We ask whether the machine is uncanny, empathetic, conscious, similar to us. Meanwhile, behind that face, vendors, human labor, police, datasets, contracts, labels, procurement, legal exceptions, and preventive translations of bodies into administrable categories are moving. The automaton becomes cover for the warehouse. Not because it is irrelevant, but because it is too visible in the wrong way.

The residue also changes function inside this history.

In the previous chapter, the residue was the wet cardboard that proprietary packaging dumps offstage. Here the point needs tightening. Not every residue is proof. A material residue can preserve friction: packaging, error, dirty path, possibility of appeal. But it can also be captured. An old slug remains navigable, keeps a historical smell, inherits trust, and meanwhile custody has changed, the archive is incomplete, the forum does not exist. What remains no longer accuses the capture; it sells it as continuity.

So there is a probative residue, when it preserves path, custody, and possibility of contestation. There is an erased residue, when efficiency cleans the corpse and leaves only the final state. And there is a trained residue, dirtier because more presentable: it survives enough to reassure, not enough to appeal. It is the old name put back to work against its own past. It is the automaton of memory: recognizable form, dead forum.

The contemporary scanner loves this third residue. It lets the scanner say that nothing has really been lost. Identity remains. The register remains. The category remains. The brand, the slug, the signature, the procedure, the dashboard remain. Only the possibility of tracing back to the touch has been emptied out. Small detail, again. The corridor is a machine made of small details that keep the culprit away.

At this point the genealogy of the tool is visible in full. An escape succeeds and reveals a crack. The crack is formalized. Formalization attacks performance while pretending to protect it. The custodian becomes too dangerous because they know. Knowledge is stratified. Verification turns into disassembly. Disassembly produces telemetry. Telemetry produces perceptual relief. The residue is erased, or left alive in a form trained not to accuse anymore.

No passage requires an absolute villain. This is the worst part. Every passage can be defended locally: security, scalability, responsibility, reduction of arbitrariness, audit, reliability, user experience. The corridor is not strong because it always lies. It is strong because it tells small truths at the point where a larger truth would be needed.

The scanner, then, is not neutral technology. It is a political solution to the problem of judgment: verify without listening, control without understanding, select without exposing itself to the speech that could change the criterion. Parrhesia is not repressed with a gag. It is disassembled into non-dangerous components, retained only where it becomes metric, discarded where it remains risk.

The next promise will be transparency.

They will tell us that seeing the scanner better is enough: open the logs, explain the model, certify provenance, sign every passage. Some of these things are useful. Without record, custody, and forum, all that remains is faith in the machine, and faith in the machine is a poor religion with a worse help desk.

But if the genealogy is this, transparency is not enough. It can illuminate the device after the device has already decided which world is visible. It can show us with precision the path of what entered and leave us mute before what had no form with which to enter.

The question, then, is not yet how to make the scanner more transparent.

It is who taught the scanner what deserves to be seen.
